--------------------------------------------------------------------------
BASELINE COMPARISON: LOGISTIC REGRESSION vs COX PH
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
n = 915,067 spells, 506,943 events, 44.60% right-censored.
Fixed horizon for the classifier: 180 days.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. The censoring problem, in counts
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
At the 180-day horizon each spell is one of:
  churned before the horizon (known churn)    :   427,375  (46.70%)
  observed past the horizon (known survivor)  :   362,622  (39.63%)
  censored BEFORE the horizon (status UNKNOWN):   125,724  (13.74%)

That last group is the whole problem. A logistic model must either
drop them (losing data, and not at random) or call them non-churners
(a known-false label). The Cox model uses every one of them as
'survived at least this long', which is exactly what was observed.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. Estimating P(churn by day 180) three ways
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Kaplan-Meier (uses censored rows correctly) :  48.62%  (95% CI 48.52-48.73)
  Naive label  (censored relabelled 'no churn'):  46.70%   -> -1.92 pp bias
  Complete case (unknown-status rows dropped)  :  54.14%   -> +5.52 pp bias

The naive label under-counts churn because every user censored before
the horizon is scored as a survivor. Complete-case analysis is biased
in the other direction: dropping short-follow-up users removes
disproportionately many who had not yet had time to churn.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. Model fits
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Cox PH            : n =   915,067  (0 dropped)   C-index = 0.8199
  Logistic (naive)  : n =   915,067  (0 dropped)   AUC     = 0.8573
  Logistic (compl.) : n =   789,343  (125,724 dropped)  AUC     = 0.8656

AUC and the C-index are not the same statistic -- AUC scores a fixed-
horizon label, the C-index scores the ranking of event TIMES -- so the
numbers are not directly comparable and no winner is declared here.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. The bias is a function of the horizon you invent
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
A classifier needs a fixed horizon H. Sweep H and estimate P(churn by H)
three ways: Kaplan-Meier (correct), the naive label (censored-before-H
scored as survivors) and complete-case (censored-before-H dropped).

  H days          KM       naive  compl.case naive bias    cc bias   cc dropped
      30      34.09%      33.68%      34.52%     -0.41     +0.42       22,102
      60      39.22%      38.57%      40.76%     -0.65     +1.54       49,185
      90      42.92%      41.93%      45.41%     -0.98     +2.49       70,112
     180      48.62%      46.70%      54.14%     -1.92     +5.52      125,724
     270      53.20%      50.03%      62.47%     -3.17     +9.27      182,285
     365      56.85%      52.15%      69.24%     -4.71    +12.38      225,861
     547      63.46%      54.95%      87.10%     -8.51    +23.64      337,753
     730      67.53%      55.38%      97.61%    -12.15    +30.08      395,902

Reading this honestly:
  * At short horizons the naive bias is small (-0.41 pp at 30 days).
    Censoring here is almost entirely administrative -- it happens at the
    2017-03-31 cutoff -- so only users who signed up near the cutoff can
    be censored early, and there are few of them.
  * It grows with the horizon, reaching 12.15 pp at 730 days, because
    a longer horizon puts more users in the unknown-status bucket.
  * Complete-case is worse throughout (+5.52 pp at 180 days) AND
    discards data: 125,724 spells at 180 days, 225,861 at 365.
  * So the binary framing is defensible at a short horizon and
    progressively less so as the horizon lengthens -- and the horizon is
    an arbitrary modelling choice the survival framing never has to make.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. 'Did they churn?' is not even a well-defined question
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
The plainest binary label -- 'was this user observed to churn' -- has no
horizon. It silently means 'within 26 months' for the earliest signups
and 'within one month' for the latest. Split by signup quarter:

  cohort             n     max f/up   naive(any)   KM 30-day
  2015-Q1       41,929          820        70.9%       10.8%
  2015-Q2       88,316          730        86.3%       59.4%
  2015-Q3       93,993          639        51.4%       13.8%
  2015-Q4      118,767          547        53.6%       20.8%
  2016-Q1      111,849          455        48.3%       16.8%
  2016-Q2       72,929          364        44.5%       20.9%
  2016-Q3      118,762          273        51.1%       38.6%
  2016-Q4      194,383          181        70.7%       66.8%
  2017-Q1       74,139           89         5.9%        6.6%

  naive(any) spans 5.9% to 86.3% across cohorts. Much of that range
  is the length of the observation window rather than churn behaviour:
  the final cohort has at most 89 days of follow-up, so a churn label
  simply cannot be true for most of it yet. A model trained on this
  target partly learns the calendar. The 30-day KM column is the
  like-for-like comparison the naive label cannot construct.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
VERDICT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
* The Cox model used all 915,067 spells. The complete-case logistic
  model had to discard 125,724 of them (13.7%) as unknown-status.
* Relabelling censored users as non-churners understates 180-day
  churn by 1.9 percentage points (46.7% vs the KM estimate of 48.6%).
* Both logistic variants agree with Cox on the DIRECTION of 19 of
  21 covariate effects, so the qualitative story is robust --
  the binary framing distorts the magnitudes and the headline rate,
  not usually the sign.
* The naive bias is horizon-dependent and modest at short horizons
  (-0.41 pp at 30 days) but reaches 12.15 pp at 730 days. Reported as found:
  this dataset's censoring is administrative and mostly late, which
  makes the naive label less damaging here than the 44.6% censoring
  rate alone would suggest. Complete-case analysis is the worse of
  the two binary options at every horizon tested.
* Only the survival model answers 'when', which is the actual question:
  it yields a median lifetime and a full S(t), neither of which a
  fixed-horizon classifier can produce at all.
